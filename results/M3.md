# M3 results: the push spin can't explain, on five real pitchers

**Short version:** adding a learned push (one constant per pitch type, or a per-pitch fit) to M1's
flight lowers the median held-out plate miss in all 22 fitted pitch types on this one split, and
**14 of 17 graded pitch types pass** the rule Ryan set (a drop of at least 0.6 in, with its 95%
interval above zero). In two graded rows the interval reaches zero: King's slider (drop 0.09 in) and
Misiorowski's slider (0.68 in, lower end -0.02). The third fail is Misiorowski's four-seamer
(0.38 in). Graded medians go from 2.03–8.26 in (M1 only) to 1.49–3.15 in. This is one split of games: on
three other splits the pass count is 16, 17 and 16, and King's slider drops by a negative amount on
two of them (see "Does the split matter?").

`CLAUDE.md` asked that M1/M2 bias for the sinkers, changeups and sweepers shrink measurably. For
the 7 graded rows of those types, which all pass, the size of the mean M1 bias falls from
2.78–7.87 in to 0.13–0.57 in (91–98% removed), and the mean error in the movement of M2's
generated pitches (`knock_on.csv`) shrinks in x and z in all 7. Ohtani's sinker and sweeper, the
pitches that motivated M3, are ungraded and keep more: the sinker's bias goes from 5.31 to 3.44 in
(35% removed, 5 held-out pitches) and the sweeper's from 7.87 to 1.70 in (78%, 27 pitches). That is
not specific to seam pitches: the four-seamers lose 87–92% of their bias too. A push fitted to the
training mean removes a bias that is stable across games by construction, so this shows the bias is
stable, not that it is seam force.

Three things limit what that means:

- **The data mostly shows a fixed habit, not a per-pitch effect.** A single constant push per
  pitch type gets a median 92% of the drop. The per-pitch model beats it with an interval above
  zero in 6 of 17 graded rows, usually by a few tenths of an inch. Only two rows gain more than
  0.6 in: King's sweeper and Misiorowski's slider. This goes against the goal of a per-pitch push.
- **This does not show the push is seam force.** It is "whatever closes the miss left after M1".
  It holds a release-state artefact, M1's efficiency can absorb much of a push on the spin-force
  line, and four-seamers need a push about as large as many other types. Three of the four
  graded four-seamers pass.
- **The stretch target is only partly met.** 9 of 17 graded rows are at or under 2 in; none is
  at or under 1 in.

These numbers use M1's new lower bound on spin efficiency, 0.01. An earlier run at the old 0.20
bound gave 16 of 17; see "Why the efficiency bound moved". Ryan moved the bound after seeing that
run, and the change cost two passes.

Real results below come from `results/m3/<pitcher>/summary.csv`, `knock_on.csv` and `skipped.txt`,
and `results/m3/bound_check.csv` (`scripts/seam_bound_check.py`), with the tables generated from
those files, not typed. Pitch and game counts come from `train_seam.py`'s printed output and the
cached CSVs. Synthetic numbers are in their own section and come from
`results/synthetic/m3_controls.csv` (`scripts/seam_controls.py`).

## What was measured

- **Data:** Statcast, regular season. Ohtani 2025 (753 usable pitches, 14 games), Sale 2026
  (2,544 of 2,575, 27 games), Misiorowski 2026 (2,647 of 2,649, 30 games), and two pitchers new
  to this milestone: Sánchez 2026 (3,158, 33 games) and King 2026 (2,981, 32 games).
  Sale and Sánchez are left-handed, the others right-handed.
- **Split by game:** 70% of each pitcher's games for fitting, 30% held out, seed 0, same split as
  M1 and M2. Held-out games: Ohtani 4, Sale 8, Misiorowski 9, Sánchez 9, King 9.
- **Three variants, same held-out pitches:** **M1** (calibrated Cd and spin efficiency, no push),
  **fixed** (M1 plus one constant (x, z) acceleration per pitch type, the training mean), and
  **per-pitch** (M1 plus a ridge regression that predicts the push from what's known at the hand:
  spin axis as sin/cos, spin rate, speed, release x/z, extension). A pitch's own movement is never
  an input.
- **The push being learned:** per training pitch, the constant acceleration (ft/s²) that makes
  M1's flight land on the Statcast fit crossing at y = 17/12 ft. It acts inside the equations of
  motion, not as a shift at the plate.
- **Miss:** distance in the x–z plane between simulated and fit crossing, in inches, from the
  release point with per-game air density, as in M1.
- **Drop:** M1's median miss minus per-pitch's median miss. **`pp_vs_fixed`:** fixed's median minus
  per-pitch's median; above zero means the per-pitch slopes helped. Both intervals are 95%,
  from resampling held-out **games** (1,000 draws), because pitches in a game share air and mound.
- **Mexico City:** King's one game there first flew at 0 ft because the venue was missing. It is
  now in the table at 7,349 ft (FanGraphs, Arizona Sports), and all numbers here include it.

## The pass rule

Set by Ryan on 2026-11-04, after the no-seam control below and before any real M3 results were
run. It has not changed since.

- **Graded** means at least 30 held-out pitches and at least 2 held-out games.
- **Pass** means the drop is at least 0.6 in and the interval's lower end is above zero.

The two targets are goals, reported beside the rule, and don't decide a pass: the **realistic bar**
(as accurate as the same pitcher's four-seamer) and the **stretch** (1–2 in).

### What the table says

- **14 of 17 graded rows pass.** The biggest drops are King's sinker (5.87 in), Sánchez's sinker

- **The fails:** Misiorowski's four-seamer drops 0.38 in [+0.10, +0.66], below the floor, from an M1
  median already at 2.03 in. King's slider drops 0.09 in [-0.33, +0.49]: M3 does nothing measurable
  there. Misiorowski's slider drops 0.68 in, above the floor, but its interval reaches -0.02: a near miss,
  like the curveball the other way (next bullet).
- **The weakest pass:** Misiorowski's curveball, drop 0.70 in with a lower end of +0.01. It is

  the other way.
- **Ohtani's four-seamer passes on 4 held-out games.** Its drop (1.99 in [+1.62, +2.33]) is big,
  but the interval is thin and shouldn't be taken at face value.
- **Passing does not single out "seam" pitches.** Four-seamers drop 1.50 to 2.21 in for Ohtani, Sale
  and King. Those are the pitches M1 was meant to handle best, so this rule alone can't
  separate a seam effect from any other direction error M1 has.

## Per-pitch or fixed habit?

**Mostly a fixed habit.** The rule from the spec: if the per-pitch model doesn't beat fixed on
held-out games, say the data shows a fixed habit.

- **11 of 17 graded rows show a fixed habit** (`pp_vs_fixed` interval reaches zero or below):
  Ohtani FF*, Sale FF, SI and SL, Misiorowski CU and FF, Sánchez CH and SL, King CH, FF and SL.
- **6 of 17 show per-pitch beating fixed**, but two of those only just (lower end +0.00 for
  Sánchez SI, +0.03 for Sale CH), and the typical gain is small (median `pp_vs_fixed` across the 17
  rows: 0.14 in).
- **Two rows gain more than 0.6 in:** King ST +0.78 and Misiorowski SL +0.71. For Misiorowski's
  slider, `results/M2.md` found the spin axis moving between clusters during the season, so the
  slopes may be tracking two shapes or a drift across the year rather than a pitch-to-pitch effect.
  I did not test that, or look at which input carries the slope.
- **For changeups and sinkers a constant push does most of the work.** Changeups gain +0.02 to +0.17
  in and sinkers +0.11 to +0.39 in. Three of those six rows do beat fixed (Sale CH, Sánchez SI, King
  SI), but by small amounts.
- Per-pitch never does significantly worse than fixed in a graded row, so keeping it does no
  measured harm. It just mostly isn't earning its slopes.

## Does the split matter?

Every number above uses one 70/30 split of games (seed 0). The intervals resample games inside that
split, so they don't cover the choice of split. `scripts/seam_split_check.py` repeats the whole
evaluation on split seeds 0–3 for all five pitchers, same code, bound and air
(`results/m3/split_check.csv`).

| Split seed | Graded rows | Pass | Best graded median (in) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (used above) | 17 | 14 | 1.49 |
| 1 | 18 | 16 | 1.42 |
| 2 | 20 | 17 | 1.36 |
| 3 | 18 | 16 | 1.39 |

What holds on all four splits: King's slider and Misiorowski's four-seamer fail every time
(Misiorowski's only just on seed 3, drop 0.5996 against 0.6), and no graded row reaches 1 in. King's
slider's drop is +0.09, -0.05, +0.32 and -0.11 in, so "M3 lowers the miss in every type" is a
property of seed 0 for that type. What does not hold: the exact 14 of 17, because which types are
graded changes with the games held out, and the other fails. Misiorowski's slider fails only on
seed 0 and Ohtani's curveball only on seed 2. So read the result as "most graded types pass, the two
above never do, 1 in is out of reach", not as 14.

## Why the efficiency bound moved

M1 used to fit spin efficiency between 0.20 and 1.0. Six of 22 rows landed exactly on 0.20, so the
fit wanted less spin force than it was allowed. Ryan moved the lower bound to 0.01 on 2026-11-04,
after a review of the first results. The new floor stays positive and doesn't bind: those six fits
land at 0.072 to 0.146. Efficiency here is a fit knob, not Savant's active-spin figure.

`scripts/seam_bound_check.py` keeps the comparison: both bounds, same air (Mexico City included),
same held-out games. Rows off the old bound don't move at all (0.0 in).

What M3 appeared to gain from the old bound:

- **Two passes were the bound.** King's slider had a drop of 3.17 in and now has 0.09 in. Misiorowski's
  went from 2.32 in to 0.68 in with an interval reaching zero.
- **Most of that "drop" was repairing M1.** M1's own median improves by 2.95 in on King's slider and
  1.58 in on Misiorowski's once the bound moves, and per-pitch ends up about where it was (2.08 to
  2.21 in, 2.12 to 2.18 in). The final miss barely depends on the bound. The credit given to M3 did.
- **The largest per-pitch wins were mostly the bound, not a release-driven push.** King's slider's
  gain over fixed went from +1.60 to -0.01 in, Misiorowski's from +1.89 to +0.71, Sánchez's from
  +0.40 to +0.05.
- **Why, most likely:** with efficiency held too high, M1 applied too much spin force, in proportion
  to each pitch's spin. The ridge's slopes on spin rate and axis can undo that, which looks like a
  per-pitch effect. That reading fits the numbers and the absorbed-push row in control 3, but I did
  not inspect the slopes, so it isn't proven.

  (King, Ohtani*). That means extra drop and extra arm-side run. Changeups show the same pattern,
  smaller. The axis gap flips sign the same way for sinkers. Sweepers (King, Ohtani*) push up and
  toward the glove side. The sinker and changeup pattern is consistent with what seam-shifted-wake
  write-ups describe, but it is not proof: anything tied to the pitch's shape would mirror too,
  including the artefact in the next section.

## Synthetic controls (fake pitches, not results)

All from `make(n=1500, seed=0)`, constant air, known physics, **no measurement noise**. They are
labelled synthetic and exist only to show what the method does when the truth is known. Reproduce
with `python scripts/seam_controls.py`.

**1. With no seam force at all, M3 still "finds" a drop.** That is where the 0.6 in floor comes from.
No synthetic type sits on either bound, so moving the bound did not change these.

| Synthetic type | Drop with no seam force (in) | `pp_vs_fixed` (in) |
|---|---|---|
| CH | 0.527 | 0.020 |
| CU | 0.369 | 0.028 |
| FF | 0.267 | 0.035 |
| SL | 0.595 | 0.004 |

The drop here is real improvement against the fit, but it has nothing to do with seams: the push
is just removing a constant offset M1 has. Three cautions on the floor:

- It is an estimate from noise-free fake pitches, one arsenal, one seed. Real data could differ.
- **SL sits at 0.595, just under 0.6, so the floor has almost no margin.** On a different
  seed a no-seam type could clear it.
- Those drops had tight intervals (widest 0.034 in), so the interval test alone would have passed
  all four. The 0.6 in floor is doing the work.

**2. The learned push includes a release-state artefact.** `release_state` runs Statcast's
constant-acceleration fit back to the release point, but a real (or simulated) ball isn't under
constant acceleration, so the start velocity comes out wrong: median absolute error
0.12 / 0.24 / 0.08 ft/s in x / y / z. With the **true** Cd and efficiency and no seam force, that
alone needs these pushes to close the miss:

| Synthetic type | Spurious push x, z (ft/s²) | Miss with true physics, no push (in) |
|---|---|---|
| CU | -0.37, +1.09 | 1.56 |
| SL | -0.49, +0.54 | 0.83 |
| CH | +0.56, +0.26 | 0.68 |
| FF | +0.46, -0.19 | 0.46 |

So up to about 1 ft/s² of a learned push can be artefact, and **push direction isn't pure seam
physics**. Real pushes run 0.79 to 7.62 ft/s² in graded rows. The large ones (sinkers, 5.6 to 7.6)
can't be mostly artefact on this estimate, but the small ones (King SL 0.79, Misiorowski SL 1.19,
Misiorowski FF 1.60) are about the artefact's size, and Sale SL 2.13, Sánchez SL 2.17 and
Misiorowski CU 2.20 could be a large part artefact.

This also bears on the stretch target. About 0.6 in (median 0.645 over all types) is the synthetic
miss against the fit from a release start, even with perfect physics. A learned push can close it
on paper (the synthetic model reaches 0.002–0.014 in), but only by fitting the artefact. So a real
median well under 1 in would partly be the model matching Statcast's curve fit, not the ball.

**3. M1's efficiency can absorb a push on the spin-force line.** A constant push on that line looks
like more or less spin, so calibration soaks it up by changing efficiency, as far as efficiency can
move inside its 0.01–1.0 bounds. The synthetic CH's spin-force direction is (-0.86, +0.51) in x, z.
`cos` is the planted push's cosine with it: -1 opposes the force, +1 goes with it, 0 is square to it.

| Planted CH push (ft/s²) | cos | CH efficiency | M1 miss (in) | Per-pitch miss (in) | `pp_vs_fixed` (in) | Fitted push x, z | Pass |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| None |  | 0.815 | 0.53 | 0.003 | +0.02 | +0.24, +0.41 | no |
| (5, -4), against the spin force | -0.99 | 0.383 | 0.56 | 0.017 | +0.43 | -0.13, -0.34 | no |
| (-5, +4), with the spin force | +0.99 | 1.000 (bound) | 5.00 | 0.006 | +0.15 | -3.17, +3.27 | yes |
| (10, -8), against, doubled | -0.99 | 0.123 | 1.61 | 0.040 | +0.88 | -0.49, -1.09 | yes |
| (4, 5), off the spin force | -0.14 | 0.724 | 6.98 | 0.007 | +0.06 | +3.28, +5.52 | yes |

The (5, -4) push opposes the spin force. It moved efficiency from 0.815 to 0.383 and left M1's
miss about where it was, so that type fails the pass rule. **So a small residual doesn't prove a
pitch has no seam effect**, and this is a reason not to read Misiorowski's four-seamer fail as "no
seam effect". The same push in the other sense pins efficiency at 1.000 and is plainly visible.

Absorption has limits short of the bounds too. Doubled to (10, -8), efficiency falls to 0.123 and
stays off the bound, yet M1 still misses by 1.61 in. These planted pushes aren't exactly on the line
(cos 0.99), and a constant push isn't proportional to spin. I didn't separate those two causes.

An absorbed push isn't quite invisible either. In the (5, -4) row the fixed model leaves 0.449 in
and per-pitch 0.017 in (`pp_vs_fixed` +0.43, against +0.02 with no push): a constant push wrongly
booked as spin may scale with each pitch's spin, so the per-pitch slopes pick it up. The off-axis
row shows a visible push isn't recovered exactly either: (+3.28, +5.52) came back for a planted
(4, 5), because efficiency absorbed part of that too.

## What's still wrong

1. **The per-pitch goal isn't supported for most pitch types.** The extra push looks mostly like a
   fixed habit: 11 of 17 rows show no per-pitch gain. Only King's sweeper and Misiorowski's slider
   gain more than 0.6 in, and neither cause is established.
2. **The push isn't identified as seam force.** It mixes seam effect (if any), release-state
   artefact, and every other M1 direction error. Four-seamers needing 1.6 to 4.6 ft/s² may mean M1's
   spin direction or lift is off generally, not just for seam pitches. Separating these would
   need a joint refit of efficiency and push, which is a spec non-goal.
3. **Part of any seam push can hide in M1's efficiency.** Fitted efficiencies stay low (four-seamers
   0.48–0.72, six sliders and cutters 0.07–0.15).
4. **Sliders are the worst fit for M3.** King's and Misiorowski's fail, and their pushes are the
   size of the artefact, so M3 has little to say about them.
5. **The 0.6 in floor has almost no margin** and comes from noise-free synthetic data.
6. **1 in isn't reached.** 1.49 in is the best graded median, and eight graded rows are above 2 in.
   Misiorowski's curveball (3.15 in) and King's sweeper (2.89 in) are the worst.
7. **Ohtani can't really be judged.** Four held-out games, and five of six fitted rows ungraded,
   including the sweeper and sinker that motivated M3.
8. **M1 and M2 write-ups were not rerun after the bound moved.** Both carry a note. M1's four rows
   that sat on 0.20 are from the old bound. `results/M2.md` has no number that uses efficiency, so
   only its movement plots are from the old bound.
9. **No season drift.** Slopes are fitted across a season in which some pitches moved
   (`results/M2.md`).
10. **Nothing is saved.** Models are refit on each run; M7 will need them stored.

## Future work

- **Joint refit of efficiency and push**, to see how much of a seam push M1 is hiding.
- **Start from a better release state** (fit the flight rather than extrapolating), which would
  remove the artefact and let the floor be re-derived.
- **Repeat the no-seam control over several seeds and with noise**, to give the floor some margin.
- **Look inside the slopes:** which input carries them on King's sweeper and Misiorowski's slider,
  and whether a by-date split explains them.
- **Set a rule for M2's movement** now that the KS numbers exist.
